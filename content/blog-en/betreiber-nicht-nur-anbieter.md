There's a question that reliably produces silence in any meeting: "What AI tools are we actually using in this company right now?"

The usual answer: "Well, a few people use ChatGPT. HR has something with Copilot. And there's been some chatbot running on the website since last year..."

The follow-up question — "Is all of that in your AI inventory?" — produces even more silence.

## The common misconception

Most companies file the EU AI Act under "applies to people who develop AI." Large model vendors, specialized software providers, maybe their own data science team. Anyone who doesn't build anything themselves doesn't feel addressed.

The regulation sees it differently. It distinguishes between **providers**, who develop AI systems and place them on the market, and **deployers**, who use AI systems under their own authority. Anyone using ChatGPT at work, rolling out a Copilot license package, or running a bought-in chatbot on the company website is a deployer.

And deployers have obligations.

## What has applied since February 2025

Two areas of regulation applied early:

- **Prohibited practices** (Article 5): certain AI applications are banned in the EU regardless of who deploys them — for example social scoring by public authorities or emotion recognition in the workplace.
- **AI literacy** (Article 4): providers *and* deployers must ensure their staff have sufficient competence in using the AI systems they deploy.

Article 4 gets skipped over a lot. It isn't a recommendation — it's an organizational obligation, and it applies to every company that allows AI into day-to-day work.

## What's added from August 2026

The real turning point is 2 August 2026. From then on, the obligations for **high-risk AI systems** under Annex III apply in full, and enforcement by the competent authorities begins.

High-risk isn't some exotic edge case. Annex III covers use cases that are already running on AI in many companies:

| Use case | Classification |
|---|---|
| Pre-screening job applications | High-risk (employment) |
| Decisions on promotion or termination | High-risk (employment) |
| Creditworthiness assessment of natural persons | High-risk (access to services) |
| Risk assessment in life and health insurance | High-risk (access to services) |
| Managing critical infrastructure | High-risk |

An autonomous agent that filters job applications doesn't fall into a gray area. It falls under Annex III.

## The sequence that works

You can't meet deployer obligations while it's still unclear what's actually in use. That's why the sequence isn't negotiable:

1. **Inventory** — which AI systems are actually in use, by whom, for what?
2. **Classification** — which of these fall under Annex III, which are limited-risk systems, which are uncritical?
3. **Ownership** — who decides on deployment, changes, and shutdown?
4. **Oversight** — how is human control under Article 14 organized?

Without step one, step two is guesswork. And without classification, there's no defensible answer to whether a system is being operated legally.

## No drama, but no autopilot either

The sentence I hear most often on this topic is some version of: "The provider handles that for us." That's true for part of the documentation obligations. It is not true for the question of what you're using a system for inside your own company, who works with it, and who intervenes when it gets something wrong. That responsibility can't be outsourced.

The most useful first step is unglamorous: a list. What are we actually using? Sounds trivial. It isn't — and in practice, it's usually what triggers the first real clarity.
